# Market assessment frameworks

## Starving Crowd test (Hormozi, after Halbert)

| Test | Strong when the evidence shows | Weak when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain | Buyers act on the problem: they pay for alternatives, search for it, describe a costly trigger | Only "nice to have" talk; no spend, no triggers |
| Purchasing power | Buyers already pay a comparable amount to an alternative, and their revenue or budget covers the price | The price is a large share of their budget; lost deals on price cluster in one segment |
| Easy to target | There are associations, lists, communities, events, specific search terms or referral paths where buyers gather | Buyers have no shared place, title, or term |
| Growing | Dated counts or trends show the buyer group rising | Counts flat or falling, or no dated evidence (then *unknown*) |

Rate each strong, mixed, weak or unknown. A market weak on pain or purchasing power rarely rescues itself through marketing.

## TAM / SAM / SOM, bottom-up

Size from buyer counts times value, not from a top-down "x% of a $NB industry".

- **TAM** = all buyers who have the job × annual value per buyer.
- **SAM** = buyers this offer can serve (ICP size band, geography, channel, fit) × annual value per buyer.
- **SOM** = buyers the business can realistically win in a stated period × annual value per buyer.

Value per buyer: use the business's own average price where it exists (say so); a survey's stated spend is a second reference, labelled stated. Show both when they differ.

Bound SOM by:
- **capacity:** how many customers delivery can handle, and how fast it can grow (hiring time, production limits);
- **win evidence:** current customers, close rates, lead volume.

Never use a fixed "1% of SAM" or similar rule. If the inputs aren't there, write a range or *unknown* and the input that would fill it.

**Goal check:** customers needed = revenue goal ÷ annual revenue per customer. Compare with SOM and capacity. If the goal exceeds them, the gap is a market, price or capacity decision, not a marketing one.

## Porter's Five Forces

| Force | High (bad for profit) when |
| --- | --- |
| Rivalry | Many similar competitors, price competition, similar claims |
| Threat of new entrants | Low cost to start, no licences, easy to copy |
| Substitutes | Different ways to do the job are cheap and good enough (software, DIY, doing nothing) |
| Buyer power | Buyers can switch easily, compare prices openly, or are few and large |
| Supplier power | Key inputs, talent or platforms are scarce or controlled by a few |

Rate each with evidence from competitor notes, lost deals, prices and the business's own costs. Unknown is a valid rating.

## PESTLE

Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental. Include only forces that change the decision, each with a source. A law, rule or platform change that has not been checked against its official source is *unverified*: write it as a question for the owner or an adviser, never as a fact.

## Timing

| Stage | Signs |
| --- | --- |
| Early | Few providers, buyers don't have a name for it, demand has to be created |
| Growing | Buyer counts or searches rising, new entrants, claims still fresh |
| Mature | Many similar providers, price pressure, claims repeated by everyone |
| Declining | Buyer counts falling, providers leaving |

## Blue Ocean (Kim and Mauborgne): an opening, not a decision

If the evidence shows every provider competing on the same factors (speed, price, reporting), note which factors could be raised, reduced, created or eliminated for this buyer. Hand it to `06-positioning`; positioning owns the choice.
