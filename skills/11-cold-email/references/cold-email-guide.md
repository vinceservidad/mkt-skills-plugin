# Cold email guide

## Account tiers

| Tier | Who | Email |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Best fit to the ICP and a dated public signal tied to the problem (a hire, a new office, a public question about the problem) | Written one at a time around that signal |
| 2 | Fits the ICP, no useful signal | A shared angle from the segment's pain, with true account details only |
| Removed | Fails a disqualifier (size, location, segment, wrong buyer) | Not written to; reason recorded |

A signal is evidence, so keep its source and date. An old or unrelated signal (an award, an acquisition by a group with its own finance team) is not a reason the buyer feels the problem today. Never invent one.

## The four-part email

1. **First line.** True and about them: their signal, or a plain statement of the situation the segment is in. Not a compliment, not "I hope this finds you well".
2. **Value.** One problem, in the buyer's words (Voice of customer), and what changes when it is fixed. One email, one idea (Rule of One).
3. **Proof.** One fact from Proof stack or `inputs/` that the owner may use in outreach. Named clients and quoted reviews need permission. If no usable proof exists, use the mechanism or the founder's own story instead.
4. **Soft ask.** An interest question the reader can answer in one word ("Worth a look?" style), not a booked 30-minute call. Offer a resource only if it exists.

Plain text, short, a real sender name, no images, no attachments, a subject line that describes the email honestly.

## Follow-ups

Each follow-up adds something new: a second angle, a resource that exists, a one-line close-the-loop. Stop at the first reply (any reply), opt-out, bounce or out-of-office that names a leave. Space touches by days, not hours. No invented trigger such as "they opened it three times".

## Deliverability (what to plan, not thresholds)

- A domain that has never sent cold email has no sending reputation. A large first-day volume risks spam placement for the business's normal mail too.
- Authenticate the sending domain (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) before sending.
- Choose: the main domain at a very low volume, or a separate sending domain or mailbox that the owner buys and sets up. Both are owner decisions; buying a domain costs money.
- Start low and raise volume only while bounces and spam complaints stay low. Verify addresses before sending.
- No tracking pixels or link shorteners by default: they hurt placement and opens are unreliable anyway.
- Mailbox providers (Google, Yahoo, Microsoft) publish sender requirements, including unsubscribe and complaint-rate rules. They change. Cite the current page with URL and date, or mark *to confirm*. Where to check: Google's email sender guidelines (support.google.com/a/answer/81126), Yahoo Sender Hub (senders.yahooinc.com).

## The law (verify, do not state from memory)

- United States: the CAN-SPAM Act covers commercial email, including business-to-business. The FTC's compliance guide lists the duties (honest header and subject, identify the message as an ad where required, a valid physical postal address, a clear opt-out honored promptly). Check ftc.gov for the current guide and date it.
- Outside the US, rules can require consent or a legitimate-interest basis even for business email (for example UK PECR and UK GDPR, EU GDPR and national e-privacy laws, Canada's CASL). If an account is outside the owner's country, flag it; often it is outside the ICP anyway.
- Personal data: keep only role, business email and the account facts needed; record the source of each contact.
- Flag the plan for qualified legal review. This is not legal advice.

## Metrics

Judge per accounts contacted: replies, positive replies, qualified meetings, clients. Record counts. Opens are distorted by privacy features and bots and are not a result. Industry reply-rate figures are not evidence about this list.
