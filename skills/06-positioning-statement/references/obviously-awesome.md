# Obviously Awesome, step by step

April Dunford's order matters: each component is decided from the one before it. Starting from the category or the tagline produces a position the evidence can't hold.

| Step | Question | Evidence that answers it | Trap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Competitive alternatives | What would our best customers do if we didn't exist? | lost-deal reasons, switch stories, what customers used before, competitor notes | listing the companies you worry about instead of what buyers actually do (often nothing, a spreadsheet, a hire) |
| 2. Unique attributes | What do we have that the alternatives lack? | the offer as delivered today; dated competitor claims | counting proposed features; calling an attribute unique when a competitor's page claims it |
| 3. Value | What does each attribute enable for the customer? | customer words, struggling moments, outcomes they report | features without a *so that*; value only the founder cares about |
| 4. Best-fit customers | Who cares most about that value? | ICP, segments, best and worst clients, churn and wrong-fit losses | *everyone who needs X*; leaving out who it is not for |
| 5. Market category | What frame makes the value obvious? | the words buyers use when they search and ask | picking a category for vanity; inventing one without the cost case |

Then the relevant trends (only with evidence that buyers feel them) and the statements.

## Labelling an attribute

- **Unique:** no alternative claims it in the dated evidence. Write *as observed on <date>*; pages change.
- **Shared:** an alternative claims it. It may still matter to buyers, but it cannot carry the position alone.
- **Better, with proof:** both have it, and a number or a buyer's reason shows ours is better *for this customer* (for example a five-day close against a stated ten business days). A claim on a competitor's page is their claim, not a measured fact.
- **Unproven:** we say it; nothing shows buyers notice or value it.
- **Unknown against competitors:** no dated evidence about the alternatives on this point. Never fill the gap with what competitors in the category *usually* offer.

## Choosing the category frame

| Option | When it fits | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Head-on in an existing category | buyers already search for the category and you can win as they define it | you are judged on the category's existing criteria |
| Subsegment (*X for Y*) | you win only for a specific customer, and that customer is identifiable | a smaller market; you must keep the *for Y* visible |
| New category | no existing frame makes the value understandable | slow, expensive market education; hand to category-design |

Evidence for the frame: the questions buyers ask on calls, the terms they search, the alternatives they name. A buyer who asks *do you work with agencies like us?* is shopping in a subsegment they already understand.

## The statements

- **Moore:** *For [target customer] who [need or opportunity], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [primary alternative], it [primary difference].* Use the primary alternative from step 1, not the competitor with the biggest ad budget.
- **Neumeier only-ness:** *[Brand] is the only [category] that [difference] for [customer] in [market] who [need] during [era].* If you can't write *only* truthfully, don't write it; say what would need verifying.
- **Ries and Trout:** a position is a word or idea owned in the mind. If the evidence shows a word buyers already associate with the business (from reviews or calls), name it.
- **Perceptual map:** two axes buyers care about, from evidence (for example *speed of numbers* and *depth of advice*). Place each alternative only from evidence; otherwise *unknown*. A map drawn from opinion is decoration.
- **USP (Reeves):** one specific benefit the alternatives cannot or do not claim.
- **Value Proposition Canvas (Osterwalder):** customer jobs, pains, gains ↔ products, pain relievers, gain creators; one line each in the section.

## Testing a position

Positioning is a hypothesis until buyers react to it. Useful tests: use the statement's framing on the next set of discovery calls and log whether buyers still ask the same clarifying question; track win rate against the primary alternative; check whether lost-deal reasons change. Name the number of calls or weeks and what result would change the position.
