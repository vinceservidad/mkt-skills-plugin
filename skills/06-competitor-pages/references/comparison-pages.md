# Comparison and alternative pages

## Page types

| Type | Reader | Core question | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Us vs X* | comparing two shortlisted options | which one fits me? | turning it into an attack page |
| *Alternatives to X* | unhappy with X, or checking before buying it | what else is there, and when is each right? | listing only yourself; inventing X's weaknesses |
| *Us vs DIY / a hire / doing nothing* | weighing whether to buy at all | is it worth paying for? | inventing the cost of doing nothing; use the buyer's own stories |
| *Best options for <buyer>* | early comparison of a category | which options fit someone like me? | ranking competitors on facts you don't have |

The status quo (doing nothing, a spreadsheet, the existing accountant) is often the biggest alternative; a *vs DIY* page can matter more than a *vs competitor* page.

## Structure that earns trust

1. **Headline and intro** naming the decision the reader is making, in their words.
2. **Short answer first:** *choose us if…; choose X if…*. Make it true for both sides.
3. **Comparison table** on the criteria the target customer actually uses (from the buyer's questions and the differentiation), not on criteria chosen because you win them. Cells hold dated facts, *not shown* or *unknown*.
4. **Our case:** the position, the mechanism and the proof, from context.
5. **When X is the better fit:** said plainly. This is the positioning trade-off made visible, and it is what makes the rest believable.
6. **Switching or next step:** real offers and terms only.
7. **FAQ:** only questions buyers asked, answered from context.

## Rules for competitor facts

- **Dated and sourced.** Every competitor fact carries where it was seen and when. Pages change: anything seen once, or before the newest observation, goes on the *Verify before publishing* list.
- **Their claims stay theirs.** *Says it is trusted by 150+ agencies (their homepage, 2026-09-25)*, not *trusted by 150+ agencies*.
- **Not shown is not the same as not checked.** *Not shown* means the page was looked at and the item wasn't there; *not checked* means nobody looked. Never turn an unchecked item into *none*. An inferred absence (*not listed on the pages seen*) counts as not checked on the page; it belongs on the verify list.
- **Not shown is a fact.** If a competitor's price isn't on its site, the page says *not published* or *contact them*. Never estimate it.
- **Weaknesses come from buyers.** A weakness is a reason buyers gave for leaving or rejecting the option (a review, an interview, a lost-deal reason), with its source and how many said it. One buyer's experience is one buyer's experience.
- **Concede what they win.** If they are cheaper, faster on something, or better for a segment, say so.
- **Names identify, nothing more.** Use the competitor's name to identify it. No logos, no implied partnership, no screenshots unless the owner clears them.
- **Owner and legal review.** Comparative claims about named companies are reviewed by the owner, and by a lawyer where the business normally uses one, before the page goes live. The skill does not publish.

## Search and platforms

A comparison page often targets searches like *X vs Y* or *X alternatives*. Use search observations only as dated evidence of what was seen (for example a keyword tool export with its date). Do not assert how a search engine ranks, rewards or penalises comparison pages, or what an AI answer engine will show; those are current platform facts that need dated first-party documentation. Hand search intent and placement to the SEO skill.

## Traps

- A table full of ticks for us and crosses for them, where the crosses are guesses.
- *Unlike other providers, we…* when only one provider was checked.
- Using lost-deal counts that aren't attributed to that named competitor.
- A *cheaper* or *faster* claim without the competitor's dated, published figure.
- Fake balance: a *when X is better* section that names no real case.
